# Ship passkeys now, keep confidential payments on devnet

As of November 2026 the two features are at very different stages. **Passkeys are mature, live on Sui mainnet, and usable today.** **Native confidential transactions are still a devnet-only, unaudited public beta**, and no testnet launch has been confirmed. Passkeys (SIP-9, signature flag `0x06`) reached mainnet at protocol v90, including passkeys inside multisig, and Sui announced them on 2025-08-07. `@mysten/sui` 2.19.0 ships `PasskeyKeypair`, though the class is still tagged `@experimental`. Sui's "Confidential Transfers" have been on devnet since 2026-06-08 with testnet "targeted later in 2026". They hide amounts and balances only; sender and receiver stay public. Issuers opt in per token and hold auditor keys. No audited third-party shielded pool exists on Sui. For ReeF, the practical answer is that passkeys are worth adding, but only after three decisions:

- **A custom domain**, because `vercel.app` is a public suffix.
- **Moving mobile off Expo Go** to dev-client/EAS builds.
- **An address model.** A zkLogin+passkey multisig is a *new address*, so existing users need an owned-object migration.

Enoki's backend `sender` sponsorship mode, which ReeF's sponsor endpoint already uses, should sponsor passkey and multisig senders, but nobody has documented or tested that. On privacy, the defensible work ReeF can ship today is Seal-encrypted payment memos and pay-in-DM. Confidential Transfers belong in a devnet prototype, and mixers should be avoided outright, given Nigeria's ISA 2025 and the unresolved Tornado Cash case.

## Passkeys have been on mainnet for over a year

Sui's passkey support is a native authenticator, not a smart-contract wallet. SIP-9 is in Final status. It defines flag `0x06` and a serialized signature of `0x06 || BCS(PasskeyAuthenticator{authenticatorData, clientDataJson, userSignature})`, where the inner signature is a secp256r1 compact `r||s` plus a 33-byte compressed public key ([SIP-9](https://github.com/sui-foundation/sips/blob/main/sips/sip-9.md)). The protocol config changelog gives the rollout history: devnet at v52, testnet at v75, passkey-in-multisig on testnet at v76, and **"Enable passkey and passkey inside multisig for mainnet" at v90** ([sui-protocol-config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/crates/sui-protocol-config/src/lib.rs)). The mainnet announcement blog is dated 2025-08-07 ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/passkey-seamless-user-onboarding)). Some docs pages still say "beta on devnet/testnet" and "not supported in multisig". Those pages are out of date.

| Capability | Status Nov 2026 | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Passkey signatures (SIP-9) | **Live, mainnet** | Since protocol v90. No epoch expiry, unlike zkLogin. |
| Passkey inside multisig, incl. with zkLogin | **Live, mainnet** | Up to 10 members, weighted threshold. |
| `PasskeyKeypair` in TS SDK | Shipped, `@experimental` | Browser provider only; no React Native provider. |
| Wallet support | Thin | Nimora extension only; Slush passkey login unconfirmed. |
| Confidential Transfers (native) | **Devnet beta** | Unaudited, crypto "not finalized"; testnet "later in 2026". |
| Private-by-default stablecoins | Roadmap | Promised "sometime in 2026"; no date. |
| Third-party shielded pools | Hackathon/announced | Octopus, SuiFish; no audits or TVL found. |
| DeepBook hidden orders / dark pool | Nothing found | Matching is fully on-chain and public. |

Two protocol details matter more than they look. First, the validator compares the WebAuthn challenge to the 32-byte Blake2b digest of the BCS intent message, and the TS SDK sends that same digest. The SIP text describes a 35-byte `intent || hash` value instead. The code wins ([passkey_authenticator.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/crates/sui-types/src/passkey_authenticator.rs)). Second, **the on-chain verifier never parses the UV/UP flags, the origin, or the rpIdHash**. It only concatenates authenticatorData into the signed message. That puts the whole user-verification policy in the client and authenticator. SIP-9 warns that any code running on the passkey's origin can trigger an assertion ([SIP-9](https://github.com/sui-foundation/sips/blob/main/sips/sip-9.md)). For ReeF, XSS on the web app becomes a signing vector (the user still sees a biometric prompt). Strict CSP and no injected third-party scripts are therefore the real security boundary. No public audit specific to the passkey authenticator turned up, though Mysten cryptographers authored the SIP.

Wallet adoption is negligible. Nimora, a Chrome extension, was the launch-partner "first wallet", and Slush's documentation says nothing about passkey login ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/passkey-seamless-user-onboarding), [Slush docs](https://docs.sui.io/onchain-finance/asset-custody/wallets/slush)). The practical consequence: on Sui, passkeys are an SDK primitive that apps embed. A ReeF passkey wallet would exist only on ReeF's RP ID. Google zkLogin is different, because the same Google account and client ID always derive the same address.

## PasskeyKeypair works, but its defaults will create orphan wallets

The SDK exposes `PasskeyKeypair`, `BrowserPasskeyProvider`, a pluggable `PasskeyProvider` interface (`create()` / `get(challenge, credentialId?)`), and `findCommonPublicKey` ([SDK docs](https://sdk.mystenlabs.com/sui/cryptography/passkey)). The address is `blake2b256(0x06 || compressedPk)`, which is not the same as a raw secp256r1 address for the same key. **The authenticator returns the public key only at creation.** `getPasskeyInstance` parses it from the registration response, and Mysten's docs say to call it only on first creation. After that the app must cache the key. Otherwise it has to recover the key with `signAndRecover`: two signatures give two sets of up to four candidate keys, and their intersection is the key. One signature works too if each candidate address is then checked on-chain. Creating a new passkey always yields a **new address** ([SDK docs](https://sdk.mystenlabs.com/sui/cryptography/passkey)).

The published 2.19.0 source shows three defaults that will bite a mainstream app ([unpkg keypair.ts](https://unpkg.com/@mysten/sui@2.19.0/src/keypairs/passkey/keypair.ts)):

- `authenticatorAttachment` defaults to `'cross-platform'`, which pushes users to security keys or the QR hybrid flow. Getting Face ID or an Android platform passkey requires passing `'platform'`.
- `user.id` is `randomBytes(10)` on every `create()`, so each call silently mints another discoverable credential and another wallet.
- Only ES256 (`alg: -7`) is requested.

Version 2.8.0 added `credentialId` targeting, which 2.19.0 includes and which avoids the account chooser. The fix pattern is to persist `{credentialId, compressedPk, address}` server-side next to the profile. The public key is public data, so this is safe. Mirror it in localStorage, and check the server before ever calling `create()` for a user who already has a passkey.

The bigger UX fact concerns how passkeys fit the hot path. **Every passkey-signed transaction costs a biometric prompt.** zkLogin's ephemeral key signs silently for the whole session. A social app that prompts for Face ID on every post, follow, or like will feel broken. Passkeys therefore belong on value-moving and recovery actions, not on the D-1 hot paths or on D-2 reaction signatures.

## Domain binding and Expo Go are the real blockers

A passkey is bound to its RP ID, and **`vercel.app` sits in the Public Suffix List** ([PSL](https://publicsuffix.org/list/public_suffix_list.dat)). ReeF's RP ID could therefore only be the exact production hostname. Every `*-git-*.vercel.app` preview would be a separate registrable domain, so passkeys created on previews would never work on production. The SDK docs' own example uses `rpId: window.location.hostname`, which would quietly create per-host wallets ([SDK docs](https://sdk.mystenlabs.com/sui/cryptography/passkey)). An RP ID is permanent, because changing it orphans every passkey and therefore every passkey-derived address. Related Origin Requests (`/.well-known/webauthn`) can extend an RP ID to other origins. Clients only have to support five labels, though, and ROR is a patch, not a foundation ([passkeys.dev](https://passkeys.dev/docs/advanced/related-origins/)). The conclusion is unambiguous: **buy a permanent custom domain and make its apex the RP ID before a single passkey is created.**

Mobile carries the bigger workflow cost. ReeF's mobile app is deliberately built for Expo Go: `apps/mobile/lib/crypto-polyfill.ts` polyfills `getRandomValues` from `expo-crypto` and AES-GCM from `@noble/ciphers` specifically to avoid a custom dev build. Both React Native passkey libraries are native modules. `react-native-passkeys` is an Expo module that needs Expo SDK 53+, iOS 15+, and Android `compileSdkVersion` 34+, plus `expo prebuild` ([react-native-passkeys](https://github.com/peterferguson/react-native-passkeys)). `react-native-passkey` (v3.x) targets iOS 15+ and Android API 28+ ([getclave/react-native-passkey](https://github.com/getclave/react-native-passkey)). Either one ends Expo Go for every developer and tester. Both platforms also require association files on the RP domain. iOS needs `/.well-known/apple-app-site-association` with `webcredentials.apps: ["<TeamID>.app.umbra.mobile"]` and a matching `associatedDomains` entry. Android needs `/.well-known/assetlinks.json` with the `get_login_creds` relation, and caches it for up to 24 hours ([react-native-passkeys README](https://github.com/peterferguson/react-native-passkeys)). Both files can be served from the Next.js app's `public/.well-known/` on the custom domain. Passkeys made on web and in the native app then share one RP ID and one address.

Hermes itself is not a problem. `PasskeyKeypair` imports only `@noble/curves` p256, `@noble/hashes`, and `@mysten/bcs`, and never touches `crypto.subtle`. The only browser-specific piece is `BrowserPasskeyProvider` ([unpkg keypair.ts](https://unpkg.com/@mysten/sui@2.19.0/src/keypairs/passkey/keypair.ts)). The real work is a custom `RNPasskeyProvider` that converts the library's JSON into objects shaped like `RegistrationCredential`/`AuthenticationCredential`, including a `response.getPublicKey()` that returns DER SPKI. One risk is unverified and must be tested on both platforms. Android Credential Manager returns the WebAuthn L3 `publicKey` SPKI, but iOS may return only `attestationObject`. In that case you would have to CBOR-decode the COSE key and re-encode it. Lagos-specific recovery is also uncertain. Synced passkeys survive phone loss through iCloud Keychain or Google Password Manager ([docs.sui.io passkeys](https://docs.sui.io/concepts/cryptography/passkeys)). No data was found on how much sync coverage exists on the low-end and shared Android devices common in Nigeria, and an unsynced passkey that is lost is unrecoverable.

## Multisig recovery works but changes every user's address

Sui supports exactly the recovery model the Sui blog advertises: a **1-of-2 `{zkLogin(Google via Enoki), passkey}` multisig**, with up to 10 members, custom weights, and either factor able to sign alone ([sui-types multisig.rs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MystenLabs/sui/main/crates/sui-types/src/multisig.rs), [Sui blog: zkLogin multisig recovery](https://blog.sui.io/zklogin-apple-multi-sig-update/)). The `MultiSigPublicKey` type accepts a `{ Passkey }` member, and a 1-of-2 transaction needs only one partial signature, so the user sees one prompt, not two ([SDK multisig docs](https://sdk.mystenlabs.com/sui/cryptography/multisig)). In daily use zkLogin keeps signing the hot path silently. The passkey is the way back in if the Google account, ReeF's OAuth client ID, or Enoki goes away. Mysten's docs also advise against relying on a single passkey for high-value accounts ([docs.sui.io passkeys](https://docs.sui.io/concepts/cryptography/passkeys)).

The catch is structural. **A multisig address is derived from its member set, and Sui multisig has no native key rotation.** EVM smart accounts like Coinbase's Base App can "add a passkey owner" with a recovery key ([Base docs](https://docs.base.org/base-app)). On Sui, any change to the member set produces a new address. ReeF's D-1 object model makes moving a user expensive. `Profile`, `Post`, and `FollowSet` are owned by the zkLogin address and gated on stored `owner`/`author` fields. `Registry` maps owner→profile. A plain `public_transfer` would leave `Profile.owner` stale and break `edit_post`. A migration therefore needs a package upgrade with a `profile::migrate_owner(reg, profile, new_owner, …)` that re-keys `Registry`, plus either a per-post `migrate_author` (batchable in PTBs) or a policy that leaves old posts frozen. Many other things also need re-pointing:

- coins and USDC
- DeepBook `BalanceManager` ownership
- Seal policies keyed to the old address
- messaging group memberships
- the `<handle>.umbra.sui` subname target
- Walrus blob ownership

Other users' `FollowSet` edges are keyed by the old address inside objects ReeF cannot rewrite, so the indexer must also treat a migration event as an address alias. Two gaps block even starting this work. Nobody has confirmed that Enoki exposes the zkLogin address seed needed to build the multisig member key. Nobody has confirmed that Enoki sponsors multisig senders.

There is a cheaper alternative: keep the zkLogin address as the canonical identity and add the passkey as a **separate "vault" sub-account** linked from the profile. That needs no migration, and it adds step-up security for large balances and trades. The price is two balances and no cryptographic recovery of the zkLogin identity. Both options are legitimate. What decides between them is timing. ReeF is testnet-only, so this is the cheapest moment it will ever have to decide which address is canonical.

## Enoki sponsorship should carry passkey senders unchanged

At the protocol level, sponsorship works with any signer: a passkey or multisig sender signature sits alongside the gas owner's signature. Enoki's HTTP API makes `sender` an explicit alternative to the `zklogin-jwt` header, "only supported when calling the API from a backend service using a private key" ([Enoki OpenAPI](https://docs.enoki.mystenlabs.com/http-api/openapi)). Its SDK examples sponsor a plain Ed25519 admin address ([Enoki examples](https://docs.enoki.mystenlabs.com/ts-sdk/examples)). ReeF's `services/indexer/src/sponsor.ts` already calls `createSponsoredTransaction` with `{network, transactionKindBytes, sender, allowedMoveCallTargets}` and **no JWT**, then calls `executeSponsoredTransaction({digest, signature})`. The six-step flow in CLAUDE.md therefore carries over unchanged: the client signs the sponsored `bytes` with `PasskeyKeypair.signTransaction` or a combined `MultiSig` signature. **No Enoki document confirms passkey or multisig senders**, though, so a testnet smoke test of both comes first. The fallback is a self-run gas station: a sponsor Ed25519 key on Render, `Transaction.fromKind`, `setGasOwner`, and dual-signature execution. Its hard part is managing the gas-coin pool under concurrency without equivocation, which is the problem Enoki and Shinami solve ([Shinami](https://docs.shinami.com/reference/gas-station-1)). The flow has one known abuse surface: the endpoint sponsors any `sender` that sends allowlisted calls. Per-user rate limits matter, and the sponsor route should bind `sender` to an authenticated session, for example a passkey-signed personal message. On the web, the existing pattern already makes integration simple. `apps/web/lib/gasless.ts` bypasses dapp-kit's "current wallet" and calls the Enoki wallet's `sui:signTransaction` directly. It can be generalized to `useGasless(tx, {as: 'zklogin' | 'passkey' | 'multisig'})`, with optional wallet-standard registration if the passkey should appear in `useWallets()` ([Sui docs: Wallet Standard](https://docs.sui.io/onchain-finance/asset-custody/wallets/wallet-standard.md)). Before shipping, check that the indexer's reaction verification goes through scheme-dispatching `verifyPersonalMessageSignature` rather than a zkLogin-only path.

## Confidential payments hide amounts, not the tip graph

Sui's official privacy feature is **Confidential Transfers**, a public beta on devnet since 2026-06-08 ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/confidential-transfers-public-beta)). The repo describes the mechanics ([MystenLabs/confidential-transfers](https://github.com/MystenLabs/confidential-transfers)):

- Twisted ElGamal over Ristretto255, with amounts split into four u16 limbs.
- Bulletproofs range proofs via `sui::rangeproofs`.
- Three flows: **wrap** a public `Coin<T>` into a `Pool<T>` (amount visible), confidential transfer into the receiver's pending balance followed by `merge`, and **unwrap** (amount visible).
- An issuer-set auditor key per token. The sender attaches an auditor-readable copy of each amount.

The README says it "has not been audited and should not be used in production" and that the "cryptography is not finalized". A third-party review filed a medium-severity issue about range-proof bytes being reusable across contexts ([issue #8](https://github.com/MystenLabs/confidential-transfers/issues/8)). Mysten's separate public promise of private stablecoin transfers "sometime in 2026" had no date as of May ([The Block](https://theblock.co/post/400396/mysten-labs-abiodun-says-sui-processed-over-1-trillion-in-stablecoin-volume-since-august-as-it-eyes-free-private-payments)). By August it was still described as "public beta" ([Sui blog interview](https://www.sui.io/blog/why-privacy-is-the-next-phase-of-blockchain-adoption)). Some aggregators claim private transactions are live on mainnet. They are wrong.

Three implications shape ReeF's use. First, **even when it is live, it will not hide who tipped whom**. Senders and receivers stay visible by design, so the social graph of tips remains public. Second, confidentiality is **issuer opt-in**. ReeF cannot turn it on for USDC; only Circle or the relevant issuer can. ReeF *can*, however, issue its own creator coins in confidential mode. Third, wrap and unwrap leak amounts at the boundary, and no source covers whether Enoki sponsors confidential-transfer PTBs, what they cost in gas, or whether proof generation runs on Hermes on low-end phones.

Everything else on Sui is weaker. Octopus is a HackMoney 2026 Groth16/Poseidon shielded pool with no audit or published addresses ([ETHGlobal](https://ethglobal.com/showcase/octopus-xu3qf)). SuiFish, Iron Fish's port, has been announced but not launched ([Iron Fish](https://ironfish.network/learn/blog/2025-10-30-suifish)). There is no Sui stealth-address protocol. Sui Move already has the primitives a shielded pool needs (Groth16 on BN254/BLS12-381, Ristretto255, Bulletproofs) ([Sui cryptography](https://docs.sui.io/develop/cryptography/)), so the blocker is audited circuits, anonymity-set size, and law, not missing cryptography.

ReeF's other stack pieces are not privacy layers for payments. zkLogin keeps the Google identity and the address unlinkable on-chain, but the address is stable and fully linkable. The `aud` claim is public, so every ReeF transaction is identifiable as ReeF's. Enoki's salt service can map Google accounts to addresses ([zkLogin docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/zklogin-integration/zklogin)). Seal is threshold encryption for data and is explicitly "not a privacy-preserving technology like zkLogin" ([Seal docs](https://docs.sui.io/sui-stack/seal)). DeepBook v3 matches fully on-chain with no hidden-order roadmap ([DeepBook design](https://docs.sui.io/deepbook-design)). Bluefin's Nautilus matcher publishes all inputs to Walrus, which makes it verifiable rather than private ([Bluefin docs](https://bluefin-exchange.readme.io/reference/how-does-it-work)).

The regulatory reading points the same way. Sui is building compliance-first partial privacy, with TRM Labs and Merkle Science as collaborators and issuers as "guardians" of view keys ([Sui blog](https://www.sui.io/blog/confidential-transfers-public-beta)). Nigeria's ISA 2025 brings digital assets under securities law and requires SEC approval to market them ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/fin-tech/1623238/what-the-investment-and-securities-act-2025-means-for-digital-and-virtual-assets-in-nigeria)). VASPs count as financial institutions under Nigerian AML law ([Sumsub](https://sumsub.com/media/spotlight/crypto-compliance-in-west-africa/)). Roman Storm's Tornado Cash retrial has been pushed to 2027-04-26 ([LCX](https://lcx.com/en/cryptonews/tornado-cash-developer-roman-storms-retrial-delayed-to-april-2027)). A Nautilus "tip mixer" or an in-house shielded pool would make ReeF look like a custodian or mixer. "Amounts private from the public, auditable by regulators" is the framing ReeF can defend. Nigerian treatment of confidential-transfer features specifically was not found, so get local counsel before production.

## A recommended sequence for ReeF

| # | Step | Why now / dependency | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Buy a permanent custom domain; move web prod to it; plan the RP ID as the apex | Prerequisite for every passkey; RP IDs are permanent | Small cost; preview deploys still can't share passkeys |
| 2 | Ship **Seal-encrypted tip memos** and **pay-in-DM** on existing Seal/Walrus messaging | Works today and has low regulatory risk | Hides context only; amount and parties stay public |
| 3 | Testnet smoke tests: Enoki `sender`-mode sponsorship with a `PasskeyKeypair` sender and a 1-of-2 multisig sender; confirm Enoki exposes the zkLogin address seed; confirm indexer verify handles flag `0x06` | Resolves the three unverified dependencies | If any fails, fall back to a self-run gas station or the vault model |
| 4 | **Decide the address model while still on testnet**: 1-of-2 `{zkLogin, passkey}` multisig as canonical (needs `migrate_owner`/`migrate_author` package upgrade + indexer aliasing) *or* a passkey vault sub-account | Testnet is the cheapest time to change canonical addresses | Multisig gives real recovery but costs a migration; vault is cheap but gives no identity recovery |
| 5 | Web passkey: `lib/passkey.ts` with `'platform'` attachment, server-persisted `{credentialId, pk, address}`, create-once guard, generalized `useGasless`; offer it as a post-onboarding "secure your account" step | Keeps Google-only onboarding at zero friction | Biometric prompt per passkey tx; keep hot paths on zkLogin |
| 6 | Mobile: switch to `expo-dev-client` + EAS, add `react-native-passkeys`, write `RNPasskeyProvider`, serve AASA + `assetlinks.json` from the domain | Shares the RP ID with web, so one address everywhere | Ends Expo Go; iOS SPKI extraction risk |
| 7 | Prototype Confidential Transfers on devnet (testnet when it lands) using a **ReeF-issued creator coin** in confidential mode, with sponsorship and Hermes proof timing measured | ReeF controls the issuer opt-in for its own tokens | Unaudited, non-final crypto; never ship to users until audited and on mainnet |
| 8 | Do not build mixers, shielded pools, stealth addresses, or TEE pooling; do not pursue DeepBook dark pools | Regulatory and audit exposure outweighs the benefit | Leaves tip graph and trades public |

Steps 1–3 are cheap and unblock everything, and step 4 is the one decision that gets more expensive every week. Passkeys should stay optional for Lagos users. Phones get lost there far more often than Google accounts do, so zkLogin-first with a passkey as the second factor is the right default ordering. Coinbase's own passkey wallet tells users to keep a separate recovery path for the same reason ([Coinbase help](https://help.coinbase.com/en/wallet/getting-started/smart-wallet-recovery)).

## Conclusion

Neither the cryptography nor the protocol is what limits ReeF here. The constraints are durable identifiers. A passkey welds a user's address to an RP ID, and a multisig welds it to a member set. Neither can be changed later without moving every owned object, so the domain and the canonical-address model are architecture decisions, not features. Making them during the testnet phase is nearly free, and making them after mainnet launch means a migration that touches every Profile, Post, FollowSet, BalanceManager, and Seal policy.

On privacy, Sui has picked "confidential amounts, visible parties, auditable by issuers". That matches what Nigerian regulators can accept, but it never hides the tip graph. ReeF's privacy story should therefore be honest and layered: encrypted context now through Seal, confidential amounts later through native transfers on tokens ReeF issues, and no attempt to hide who pays whom.
